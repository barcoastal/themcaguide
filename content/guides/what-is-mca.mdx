A merchant cash advance provides money to a business in exchange for an agreed amount of future receipts. You may repay through a share of card sales or withdrawals from your bank account. Before evaluating speed or approval odds, answer a more useful question: **what cash will remain for running the business after payments?**

The FTC describes MCAs as a generally higher-cost, short-term financing option and highlights concerns about disclosures and collection practices in its [small-business financing staff perspective](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2020/02/small-business-financing-staff-perspective-outlines-issues). This guide explains the mechanics; it does not determine whether a particular agreement is legally a loan.

<h2 id="read-the-numbers">Read an offer in five numbers</h2>

Ask for the advance amount, net proceeds, purchased amount or total repayment, payment schedule, and additional charges. Keep the offer and final contract together: a sales quote is not a substitute for the signed terms.

**Worked example, not a market average:** an offer lists a $50,000 advance, a 1.35 factor, and $2,000 in fees withheld at funding. You receive $48,000. Contracted payments are $50,000 × 1.35 = $67,500. The difference between cash received and payments is $19,500, before any other charges.

At a fixed $500 per business day, the balance takes 135 payment days. That is 27 five-payment weeks if there are no holidays, skipped payments, or adjustments. A calendar estimate needs the actual debit schedule. Use our [MCA cost calculator](/tools/mca-calculator) to test a sales-based payment scenario and inspect its assumptions.

<h2 id="factor-versus-apr">Why 1.35 does not mean 35% APR</h2>

The factor charge in the example is 35% of the stated advance. APR also depends on when money changes hands and how much is outstanding over time. Paying an advance back in installments reduces the outstanding economic balance. Simply dividing the fee percentage by the term does not account for that.

Our [factor-rate converter](/tools/factor-rate-to-apr) solves for a rate using a stated installment schedule. Its result is an illustrative annualized borrowing-cost estimate, not a statutory disclosure or a claim that every MCA is a loan. Compare actual offers using the same net proceeds and payment dates.

<h2 id="payment-methods">Percentage payments and fixed withdrawals differ</h2>

With a genuine percentage-of-sales arrangement, lower eligible sales produce lower collections. For example, a 12% collection on $3,500 of eligible sales is $420; on $2,000 it is $240. Those are arithmetic illustrations, not promises about your contract.

A fixed ACH withdrawal can keep taking $420 even during a slower period. A reconciliation provision may allow adjustments based on receipts, but you need to understand how to request it, what evidence is required, when it takes effect, and whether past overcollections are addressed. Ask for the process in writing before signing.

<h2 id="cash-flow-test">Test the business, not just the payment</h2>

Create a week-by-week forecast showing collections, payroll, rent, inventory, taxes, existing financing, and the proposed payment. Then reduce projected sales and delay a large customer payment. The question is whether the resulting cash balance still funds essential operations.

As an illustration, a business with $4,000 left after ordinary weekly expenses would have $1,500 remaining after five $500 withdrawals. A $2,000 decline in available operating cash would turn that cushion into a $500 shortfall. Revenue alone does not establish affordability. The [cash-flow calculator](/tools/cashflow-calculator) can help organize the inputs.

<h2 id="contract-checklist">Six questions to take to the funder</h2>

1. How much reaches my account after all fees and any existing balance payoff?
2. Which receipts count, and are payments fixed or tied to actual sales?
3. How does reconciliation work, and who handles requests?
4. What collateral, guarantees, default clauses, and court or arbitration provisions apply?
5. Does early payment reduce the purchased amount, and can I see a written payoff example?
6. What happens if revenue drops, the business closes, or a bank debit fails?

Do not assume a product advertised as unsecured has no personal guarantee or lien. The FTC has brought cases alleging misrepresentations about both [collateral and guarantees](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2020/08/protecting-small-businesses-seeking-financing-during-pandemic).
