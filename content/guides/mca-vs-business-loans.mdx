A merchant cash advance and a business loan may both put cash into the same account. The important differences appear in the agreement and in the business's cash flow after funding. Use this guide to compare written offers without relying on a factor rate, advertised interest rate, or approval speed alone.

<h2 id="structure">Start with the payment structure</h2>

An MCA is commonly documented as a purchase of future receipts, with a specified purchased amount. Payments may come from a percentage of eligible sales or estimated bank withdrawals with a reconciliation process. Read our [MCA basics guide](/guides/what-is-mca) for the difference.

A term loan typically sets principal, interest, a maturity date, and payment obligations. Products vary: an online business loan may collect daily, while many bank term loans collect monthly. For example, SBA explains that most [7(a) term loans](https://www.sba.gov/loans/7a-loans/) use monthly principal-and-interest payments. Payment frequency alone cannot tell you whether a contract is a loan or a receivables purchase.

The FTC has alleged that some MCA providers misrepresented funding amounts, collateral requirements, and guarantees. Its [financing enforcement discussion](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2020/08/protecting-small-businesses-seeking-financing-during-pandemic) is a reason to verify those terms, not evidence that every provider behaves the same way.

<h2 id="example">Worked example: a lower payment is not the full answer</h2>

Consider two hypothetical offers with $50,000 net proceeds and no additional fees. An advance requires $67,500 over 135 business-day payments of $500. A loan requires twelve monthly payments of $4,600, or $55,200 total. The difference above cash received is $17,500 for the advance and $5,200 for the loan.

That example illustrates a comparison method, not typical market pricing or available offers. The business must still check its eligibility, contractual restrictions, collateral, and whether payments fit its actual collection dates. For a weekly cash plan, the advance consumes $2,500 in a five-payment week. For the loan, budget for the full monthly withdrawal on its due date instead of assuming that a weekly average will cover it.

<h2 id="apr">Compare rates carefully</h2>

A factor of 1.35 means a 35% charge on the stated advance before other fees. It is not an APR. An annualized cash-flow rate considers the payment schedule and net funding. Even identical total charges can produce different annualized rates when payment dates change.

Our [factor-rate estimator](/tools/factor-rate-to-apr) assumes equal monthly installments. The [MCA calculator](/tools/mca-calculator) uses constant estimated daily collections and an explicit payment-frequency assumption. Neither replaces the legally required disclosure for a specific transaction. Ask the provider for a complete schedule when comparing against another quote.

<h2 id="decision">A decision checklist before signing</h2>

- Can you explain the source of repayment without relying on another advance?
- Have you tested a slower-sales period and a delayed customer payment?
- Do you know exactly what a reconciliation request requires?
- Have you reviewed liens, personal guarantees, and restrictions on other financing?
- Is the early-payoff amount documented rather than promised verbally?
- Have you compared an appropriate [alternative financing option](/guides/mca-alternatives)?

If any answer is unclear, request clarification in writing. For an unfamiliar guarantee, collection provision, or court clause, use the [attorney consultation checklist](/guides/mca-attorney-complete-guide). A product category cannot tell you whether an individual agreement is suitable for your business.
